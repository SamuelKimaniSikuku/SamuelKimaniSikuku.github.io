Two Ideas of Time

2026

The meeting starts at 14:00. The gathering starts when everyone has arrived.

Two ideas of time — and if you are African living abroad, you carry both. Our new report traces where each came from. Three findings:

1. Europe needed 500 years to learn the clock — tower bells (1300s), the pendulum (1658), factory fines (1760s–1830s), one world time (1884). Africa was given ONE generation.

2. The difference still sits in our payslips. The West: ~2,080 priced hours a year, 26 paychecks. “Unpaid time is wasted time.” Africa: 12 paydays, the month is the unit. “Time with people costs nothing.”

3. Africa’s own clocks never stopped. Kenya and Tanzania still count the day from sunrise — “hour one” is 7 a.m. Ethiopia, never colonised, keeps its own clock and a 13-month year. The Igbo built the week from four market days — and still name children after them.

And here’s the honest part: neither system wins.

African event-time gives strong community (time spent on people is never wasted), presence (elders, guests and grief get unmeasured time), and resilience (plans bend without breaking). But: friction wherever synchrony matters — flights, deadlines, deals — and a stereotype tax that the punctual still pay.

Western clock-time gives millions of strangers cooperating on schedule and fine-grained planning (the future feels bookable). But: burnout, hurry, loneliness — and every unpaid hour reads as “wasted.”

Each system is rational inside its own logic. Each has a shadow side.

So for us abroad: speak clock at work — there, it means respect. Speak event with family — some things end when they end.

You didn’t lose your sense of time. You doubled it.

Which clock raised you?
